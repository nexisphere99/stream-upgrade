I wake up to the alarm I've snoozed three times and the taste of stale Monster and the ambient dread of a person whose first conscious thought each morning is a number — viewer count, bank balance, days until rent, some metric of inadequacy that my brain serves up before I've even opened my eyes, like a concierge of suffering. Good morning, sir. Today's disappointment is already prepared.

But today I wake up smiling.

My phone is on the floor beside the mattress, screen-down, and I know before I flip it over that there are messages on it, because the last thing I did before falling asleep was send a red heart emoji to a girl who kissed me in a parked Honda Civic and the first thing my brain produces this morning isn't a number. It's a feeling. Warm, stupid, disproportionate to any rational assessment of my circumstances. The feeling of someone who was, for a few hours last night, not alone.

I put the phone down. Stare at the ceiling. The crack is still there — still growing, still splitting the plaster from kitchenette to pillow — but it looks different this morning. Less like a fault line. More like a map. A line on a surface that leads somewhere, even if I don't know where.

The apartment is the same. The mattress, the desk, the fridge, the burrito wrapper I STILL haven't thrown away. 10:45 AM. I sit at the desk. The monitors wake. The dashboard from two days ago — the morning stream, the $3.50 — is still visible on the second screen. I close it. Open a browser. Open Kira's stream page — not to watch (she hasn't gone live yet) but out of habit, the way you check the weather or the time, a reflexive verification that the world is still turning.

12,400 followers. I have 54.

I don't look at the number with jealousy this time. Not exactly. The jealousy is there — it's always there, a low hum beneath everything, the baseline frequency of a person watching someone else succeed at the thing they're failing at — but it's mixed with something new. Something from yesterday. The conversation in her apartment. The dashboard. The $487. The truth she told me about faces and algorithms and the specific architecture of an economy that rewards her existence and ignores mine.

*"If I were a guy with the exact same personality, the exact same humor, the exact same stream quality — I'd be you, Eli."*

I hear her voice saying it. I hear it clearly, perfectly, the way you hear something that imprinted itself on your brain through sheer force of importance. She said it sitting in her desk chair, three feet from me, with the ring light off and the afternoon light catching her glasses, and she said it because she's honest and because she cares and because the truth is the truth whether it's comfortable or not.

The truth is: the system rewards a specific thing. Female. Cute. On camera. Those three inputs, in that combination, produce an output that I — male, average, poorly lit — cannot replicate.

Cannot replicate as myself.

The thought arrives like a knock at a door I didn't know existed. Quiet. Polite. Inevitable. I'm sitting at my desk in a grey shirt with shampoo'd hair and a girlfriend and $47 in the bank, and the thought knocks and I open the door and what's on the other side is not a plan, not yet, but the raw material for one.

What if the input changed?

Not me. Not my skill, my humor, my content. Those are fixed — those are the things Kira said are good, the things she said would work if the system weren't rigged. The variable isn't the content. The variable is the container. The voice. The name. The gender the algorithm reads when it decides who to surface and who to bury.

I open a new browser tab. I type, slowly, deliberately, each keystroke a step further from the person I was yesterday:

"voice changer software for streaming"

The research takes ninety minutes.

I approach it the way Kira approaches her stream strategy — methodically, analytically, the way a person with a ring light and cable couture approaches a problem. Not with the midnight desperation of a man grasping at anything — with the morning clarity of a man who slept well because someone kissed him and who is now, rested and caffeinated (I made coffee, actual coffee, with the hot plate and the cheap grounds I bought three weeks ago and never opened), applying his girlfriend's market analysis to a problem she identified.

VoxMorph. That's the software. Free tier available, premium at $8/month. Real-time voice modulation — pitch shift, formant adjustment, breathiness control. OBS plugin for direct stream integration. I watch four YouTube tutorials. I read the documentation. I download it. Install it. Open it.

The interface is clean — sliders, dials, a waveform visualizer, a test button. I pull the budget mic toward me. Clear my throat.

<<eli "Testing. One, two, three.">>

My voice, through the speakers. Male. Flat. The voice of EliPlays. The voice that earned $3.50 in two hours.

I move the sliders. Pitch up. Formant up. Breathiness to 38%.

<<eli "Testing. One, two, three.">>

The voice that returns is not mine.

It's female. Soft. A little breathy, a little uncertain, with a warmth in the lower register that sounds real — not robotic, not obviously processed, but like a woman in her early twenties speaking into a microphone for the first time. There's a shimmer at the edges where the algorithm meets the organic, a faint artificiality that a sound engineer would clock but a Twitch chat scrolling at 100 messages a minute would never notice.

I adjust. Pitch down slightly — too high sounds cartoonish. Formant down a hair. Breathiness to 35%. I test again.

<<eli "Hey everyone, welcome to the stream.">>

Better. The voice settles into a range that feels lived-in, like it belongs to a person who exists. A girl. A girl named—

I open Twitch. Account creation. The username field blinks.

I type: **ElliePlayz**

Ellie. Two letters from Eli. A feminization so thin it's transparent, a costume made of gauze, and yet the moment I type it and hit enter and the account confirms, something shifts. ElliePlayz exists now. She has a username and an empty channel page and zero followers and she is, in every measurable way, exactly as much of a failure as EliPlays. But she has one thing he doesn't: the right input for the system.

I build the stream layout. No webcam — not yet, not ever, maybe. A background image: anime-style gradient, pink to purple, stars, borrowed from a free wallpaper site. The game capture. A text overlay in a cursive font — "ElliePlayz ✨" — positioned in the upper left. Chat on the right. The VoxMorph plugin routed through OBS, processing my microphone in real time.

It takes twenty minutes. The setup is crude — nothing like Kira's polished, professional layout with its branded overlays and custom emotes and animated alerts. This is a first draft. A sketch on a napkin. But it's functional. It works. The voice goes in male and comes out female and the screen shows a pink gradient and a name that belongs to no one and I am, as of 12:15 PM on a Thursday in January, ready to lie to the internet.

I think about Kira.

I think about her sitting in her desk chair yesterday, glasses off, cleaning them on her turtleneck, telling me the truth about the system. I think about the red heart she sent this morning. I think about her coming to pick me up at 6. I think about the fact that I am about to do something I cannot tell her about.

The thought produces a specific feeling — a small, sharp point of pressure behind my sternum, like a splinter driven just deep enough to be uncomfortable but not deep enough to demand attention. Guilt. Not the dramatic, crushing kind. The small kind. The kind you can work around, the kind you can reason with, the kind that fits in the corner of a room if you don't look at it directly.

*It's just an experiment. I'm testing Kira's theory. She said the system rewards a specific input — I'm testing whether she's right. It's data collection. It's market research. I'll tell her about it when there's something to tell. When I have results. When the experiment means something beyond desperation.*

The face that is, in approximately forty-five minutes, going to sit in a clinic and ask how to become a different face.
