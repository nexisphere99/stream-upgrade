# DAY 3 — "The Door" (Part 2: RegenaForm) — KIRA ROUTE

---

The clinic is not what I expected.

I expected sterile. I expected fluorescent lights and linoleum floors and the antiseptic smell of a dentist's waiting room — the aesthetic of medical discomfort, the visual language of "this will hurt but it's necessary." What I get instead is a door at the end of a hallway on the second floor of a building that looks, from the outside, like every other building on Meridian — brick, unremarkable, the kind of facade that says "accountant's office" or "insurance broker" or "nothing to see here."

Suite 12. A frosted glass door with the RegenaForm logo etched into it — the double helix in chrome, elegant, catching the hallway light like a piece of jewelry. No signage beyond the logo. No description of services. No "ENTER HERE TO HAVE YOUR FACE RESTRUCTURED." Just the helix and the suite number and the quiet confidence of a business that doesn't need to advertise because the people who need it will find it.

I push the door open.

The waiting area is small and immaculate. Hardwood floors, warm-toned, polished to a shine that makes the apartment's hardwood look like plywood. Two low couches in dove grey, the kind with precise right angles and cushions that look like they've never been sat on. A glass coffee table with a single orchid in a white ceramic pot and a stack of magazines that are not magazines — they're lookbooks, glossy portfolios of RegenaForm's work, each page a before-and-after that blurs the line between medicine and art. The lighting is recessed, warm, the kind that makes skin look good regardless of what the skin actually looks like. A faint ambient sound — not music, not quite white noise, but something between the two, a frequency that says *relax* on a level your brain processes without your permission.

And behind the reception desk: Lina.

I see her and my brain does something it rarely does — it stops. Full halt. No internal monologue, no anxious chatter, no running calculation of my inadequacies. Just: stop. Look. Process.

She's beautiful. Not in the way Kira is beautiful — Kira's beauty is specific, angular, made of sharp bobs and wire frames and the intelligence behind her eyes. Lina's beauty is... universal. Impossible to categorize. She could be any ethnicity, all of them, none of them — honey-toned skin that glows like it's generating its own light source, large doe-shaped eyes in an amber-gold that I've never seen in a human face outside of colored contacts, soft wavy hair in caramel and brown past her shoulders, full lips in a smile that's warm and practiced and slightly, almost imperceptibly, too perfect. She's wearing a fitted dove-grey dress with the chrome double-helix embroidered on the chest, and everything about her reads as "advertisement." Not in a cheap way. In the way that a luxury car in a showroom window is an advertisement — the product is its own pitch.

<<lina "Welcome to RegenaForm! You must be Ellie.">>

Her voice matches. Melodic. Modulated. Every syllable placed like a note in a composition.

<<eli "Uh. Yeah. Hi.">>

<<lina "Wonderful. Dr. Voss is expecting you. Can I get you anything before your consultation? Water, tea, sparkling?">>

<<eli "I'm fine. Thanks.">>

<<lina "Of course. Please have a seat — the doctor will be right with you.">>

I sit on the grey couch. It's as uncomfortable as it looks — beautiful but rigid, designed for aesthetics rather than human bodies. I pick up one of the lookbooks. The pages are heavy stock, matte finish, each spread showing a transformation: the left page a "before" shot, the right an "after." The changes range from subtle — a jawline softened, a nose refined, skin cleared — to dramatic. Full facial feminization. Body contour. Results that look less like surgery and more like a person being translated into a different language of themselves.

I'm looking at a spread showing a jaw transformation — angular-masculine to soft-feminine, the bone structure literally reshaped — when a door opens at the back of the waiting area and a person steps through.

Dr. Maren Voss.

They're — and I use "they" because my brain can't settle on a pronoun and neither, it seems, can Dr. Voss's face — tall, lean, somewhere in their mid-thirties, with the kind of bone structure that exists in the exact center of a spectrum that most people live at the edges of. Sharp cheekbones but soft mouth. Strong brow ridge but arched, feminine eyebrows. Ash-blonde hair pulled back sleekly from a face that is striking precisely because it refuses to be categorized. They're wearing a tailored white lab coat over a black high-collared shirt, slim black trousers, minimalist white shoes. A thin silver bracelet on the left wrist. The RegenaForm helix pin on the lapel, catching light.

Their eyes are cool grey-green. Steady. The kind of eyes that don't look at you — they *read* you. Like a barcode scanner in a face.

<<dr_maren "Ellie. I'm Dr. Maren Voss. Welcome.">>

The voice is measured, calm, with an accent I can't place — something European, maybe Scandinavian, smoothed by years of professional neutrality into a register that gives nothing away.

<<eli "Hi. Thanks for fitting me in today.">>

<<dr_maren "Of course. Shall we?">>

They gesture toward the door they emerged from. I follow them through.

---

The consultation room is a continuation of the waiting area's aesthetic — warm lighting, wood and glass, the clinical minimized in favor of the comfortable. There's no examination table. Instead, two chairs facing each other across a low table, with a large screen mounted on the wall behind Dr. Maren's chair. It looks more like a design studio than a medical office.

I sit. Dr. Maren sits across from me. They cross one leg over the other — an elegant, controlled motion — and fold their hands in their lap. The grey-green eyes settle on my face with the attentiveness of someone cataloging its features.

<<dr_maren "So. Tell me what brings you here.">>

I've rehearsed this. On the walk over, in the elevator, in the waiting room. I've assembled a script that sounds reasonable, practical, the kind of thing a person might say without sounding like a person who's pretending to be a woman on the internet for money.

<<eli "I'm a streamer. Online content creator. I'm looking to... upgrade my appearance for camera. Just — small things. I want to look a little more polished. Maybe a bit more... feminine? Not — not like a full transformation. Just camera-friendly.">>

I hear myself say it and it sounds almost normal. Almost like a reasonable request from a person who works on camera and wants to look better. Dr. Maren's expression doesn't change — the same steady, neutral attention, the barcode scanner reading.

<<dr_maren "I see. Can you be more specific about what you'd like to change?">>

<<eli "I'm not sure, honestly. I don't know what's possible. I just know that my face... isn't doing what I need it to do.">>

<<dr_maren "And what do you need it to do?">>

<<eli "...make people stop scrolling.">>

Something shifts in Dr. Maren's expression. Not a smile — they don't smile, exactly — but a softening. A recognition. As if "make people stop scrolling" is a diagnosis they've heard before, a presenting symptom they know how to treat.

<<dr_maren "May I?">>

They gesture toward my face. I nod, unsure what I'm agreeing to. They lean forward and — with the precise, impersonal care of a jeweler examining a stone — they turn my head gently with two fingers on my chin. Left. Right. Center. Their touch is cool and dry and completely devoid of warmth or hesitation. Professional in the truest sense: my face is an object they're assessing, not a person they're touching.

<<dr_maren "Good bone structure beneath the surface. The jawline has potential for significant feminization with minimal intervention. The brow ridge is low — that's actually advantageous. Your skin responds well to treatment. Eyes are proportionally balanced. Nose would benefit from refinement but isn't a priority.">>

They're describing my face the way an architect describes a building they intend to renovate — noting the foundation, identifying the load-bearing walls, planning what to preserve and what to strip away.

<<dr_maren "Let me show you something.">>

They turn to the wall-mounted screen. A few taps on a tablet and my face appears — captured by a high-resolution camera I didn't notice built into the frame of the screen. The image is sharp, mercilessly detailed. Every pore, every blemish, every asymmetry. My face at a resolution I've never seen it in, exposed under clinical lighting, and looking at it is like hearing a recording of my own voice — familiar but wrong, the same and not the same, the private made public.

Dr. Maren touches the screen. A digital overlay appears on my face — lines and contours, like an architectural blueprint, mapping the bone structure beneath the skin.

<<dr_maren "Here's what I'm seeing.">>

They draw with their finger. Lines appear.

<<dr_maren "The masculine markers are concentrated here — the brow ridge, the jaw angle, the nose width. These are the features that code 'male' to the viewer's eye, usually within the first half-second of seeing a face. Everything else — your eye shape, your lip proportions, your cheekbone placement — is actually quite neutral. You're closer to the center of the spectrum than you think.">>

<<eli "Closer to the center of what spectrum?">>

<<dr_maren "The gender presentation spectrum. Your face reads male because of four or five specific features. Remove or soften those, and the rest is... ambiguous. Open to interpretation. Camera-friendly, as you put it.">>

They tap the screen again. The overlay shifts — the digital lines redraw, and my face changes. Not dramatically. Subtly. The jaw softens. The brow smooths. The lashes lengthen. The skin clears. The face on the screen is still recognizably mine but also recognizably *different* — like a relative I've never met, a cousin who inherited different genes from the same pool.

I stare at it.

<<dr_maren "This is achievable with Tier 1 modifications alone. Facial feminization. Eyelashes, brow sculpting, skin refinement. No structural bone work, no implants, nothing invasive. The procedures are DNA-based — we use your own genetic material, so there's no rejection risk, no scarring, full sensory preservation. Recovery time for each feature is six to twelve hours.">>

<<eli "And the cost?">>

<<dr_maren "Depends on the selections. The full Tier 1 menu ranges from 500 to 4,500 Dollars. Individual features are priced separately. We also offer... introductory packages.">>

They say "introductory packages" the way a dealer says "first one's free" — casually, offhandedly, as if the generosity is routine and not calculated.

<<dr_maren "For new clients, we offer a complimentary eyelash enhancement — our most popular introductory procedure — and for streamers specifically, a professional-grade wig from our cosmetics line. No charge. Consider it... a sample.">>

A sample. A taste. The thing that gets you through the door and seated in the chair, the thing that lets you see the results on your own face in your own mirror, the thing that makes the next tier not just possible but inevitable.

I know what this is. I can see the funnel — the marketing, the psychology, the escalation ladder. First visit free. First modification free. Then the brow sculpt for 300 $. Then the nose for 500. Then the jaw for 800. Each step a little more, each result a little better, each number on the dashboard a little higher, until you've climbed the ladder so far that looking down would mean seeing a person you no longer recognize as yourself.

I know what this is. And I say yes anyway.

<<eli "What would you recommend? For... starting?">>

Dr. Maren's grey-green eyes meet mine. Steady. Reading.

<<dr_maren "The complimentary eyelash enhancement — Natural-Long variety. Dense, dark, naturally long. Subtle enough that most people won't identify the change consciously, but the effect on camera is significant. Lashes frame the eyes. Framed eyes hold attention. Attention is the currency you're trading in.">>

<<eli "And beyond that?">>

<<dr_maren "If you'd like to invest in one paid procedure today, I'd recommend the brow sculpt. Feathered — soft, natural-looking, a slight feminine arch. It opens the eye area, reduces the masculine coding of the brow ridge, and pairs beautifully with the lash enhancement. Three hundred Dollars.">>

300 $. Which converts to — I do the math — roughly $300 in real currency. Which I don't have. Which I absolutely don't have. My bank account has $47 plus the $3.50 from yesterday that hasn't cleared yet. I cannot afford a $300 cosmetic procedure. I cannot afford a $30 cosmetic procedure. I cannot afford the coffee Marcus bought me two days ago.

But.

ElliePlayz made $47 in one hour. One stream. If the second stream tonight does even half as well — and the numbers suggest it will, given the follower base and the algorithm momentum — that's another $25-30. Tomorrow, another stream. Next week, multiple streams. The math isn't theoretical. It's already proven. The system works. The voice works. The money is real.

I can pay for this. Not today. But soon. Very soon.

<<eli "Can I do a payment plan?">>

<<dr_maren "We offer credit through our internal system. Dollars-based. The eyelashes are complimentary today. The brow sculpt — 300 $ — can be billed to your streaming account with a seven-day payment window.">>

<<eli "My streaming account?">>

<<dr_maren "ElliePlayz, correct?">>

I freeze. I didn't — I didn't give them that name. The consultation form said "Ellie." Just Ellie. No channel name, no streaming details. But Dr. Maren says "ElliePlayz" like they've been watching, like they know, like the information arrived through a channel I can't see.

<<eli "How did you—">>

<<dr_maren "We keep our ear to the ground. New streamers with... potential... tend to find their way here. It's not unusual. Many of our most successful clients began exactly where you are.">>

They say it smoothly. Too smoothly. The words are a bridge over a question I should be asking — *how do you know my stream name?* — but the bridge is well-built and the far side has a brow sculpt and eyelashes waiting, and I cross it without looking down.

<<eli "Okay. Let's do the eyelashes and the brow sculpt.">>

<<dr_maren "Excellent. And the wig — our gift to you. We have a range specifically designed for camera use. Natural appearance, adjustable fit, fiber that responds to lighting like real hair. Our coordinator will help you select one after the procedure.">>

They stand. Extend a hand. I shake it — their grip is firm, dry, the handshake of someone who shakes hands the way they do everything else: with precision and intent.

<<dr_maren "If you'll follow me.">>

---

The procedure room is behind another frosted glass door, and it's the first space in the clinic that actually looks medical — a reclining chair that splits the difference between dental and spa, adjustable lighting rigs, a tray of instruments I can't identify, and a screen showing my face again, this time with the target areas highlighted. The eyelashes in blue. The brows in green.

I sit in the chair. It reclines smoothly — none of the hydraulic wheezing of my desk chair, just a silent, engineered glide backward until I'm staring at the ceiling, which is featureless and white and nothing like the crack in my apartment ceiling, which I find oddly disorienting. My ceilings should have cracks. That's the deal.

<<dr_maren "The eyelash procedure takes approximately twenty minutes. The brow sculpt, thirty. You'll experience mild warmth at the application sites — that's the bioactive compound integrating with your follicular DNA. No pain. No downtime. Results are immediate.">>

<<eli "Immediate?">>

<<dr_maren "The modifications grow from your own genetic template. We're not attaching anything — we're activating dormant potential in your existing cells. The lashes will be fully extended by the time you leave the chair. The brows will reshape over the course of two to three hours.">>

Activating dormant potential. I wonder if they use that phrase deliberately — if it's medical terminology or marketing copy, if there's a difference, if it matters. The idea that my cells contain the blueprint for feminine eyelashes, dormant, waiting to be switched on — it's either science or poetry and both are doing something to the inside of my chest.

They apply something to my eyelids. Cool, gel-like, with a faint chemical tang that doesn't smell medical but doesn't smell *not* medical. The warmth follows — gradual, spreading, not unpleasant. Like someone pressing warm fingertips against the skin around my eyes.

<<dr_maren "You can close your eyes. The process works best with the lids relaxed.">>

I close my eyes. The warmth deepens. I can feel something happening — not movement, not growth, but a *stirring*. A cellular-level activity that registers as a hum at the edge of perception, like hearing a sound just below the threshold of hearing. My lashes tingle. The skin around my eyes tightens slightly, then relaxes.

Twenty minutes. I lie there with my eyes closed and I think about Kira.

I think about her sitting in her desk chair yesterday, explaining click-through rates, the afternoon light on her glasses. I think about the pixel-heart tattoo beneath my palm when our fingers interlaced at the izakaya. I think about the kiss — the glasses bumping my nose, the seatbelt catching, the lip gloss. I think about the red heart she sent this morning and the one I sent back.

I think about the fact that I'm lying in a chair in a clinic four blocks from my apartment having my eyelashes modified by a doctor with no discernible gender, using money I don't have, earned by pretending to be a woman, hidden from the girlfriend I've had for approximately 36 hours.

The splinter in my chest pushes a little deeper.

<<dr_maren "Eyelashes complete. Opening your eyes now.">>

I open them. The ceiling is still white. Still crack-free. Dr. Maren holds a mirror up — a real mirror, clinical-grade, the kind that shows everything.

My eyes look different.

The lashes are — they're mine. They look like they grew from my face, because they did, because that's how this technology works. But they're longer. Darker. Denser. A fringe of dark lashes that frames my eyes the way a frame holds a painting, creating a border that draws attention inward, toward the brown irises, toward the look in them, toward *me*. The effect is subtle and enormous simultaneously — if you weren't paying attention you'd miss it, but if you were looking at my face on a stream, on a webcam, in a thumbnail, your eyes would catch on the lashes the way eyes catch on movement. They're an anchor. A reason to look twice.

<<dr_maren "Natural-Long. Dense, dark, no maintenance required. They'll grow at the same rate as your natural lashes and can be trimmed if desired, though I wouldn't recommend it. The length is optimized for camera distance.">>

I stare at my reflection. The face is still mine. Still the pale skin, the dark circles, the acne, the wide-bridge nose, the thin lips. But the eyes are different. The eyes look — not feminine, not yet, but *interesting*. Like something changed in the equation and the result shifted, and the shift is small but visible, like a star moving in the sky.

<<eli "...wow.">>

<<dr_maren "Shall we proceed with the brows?">>

<<eli "Yeah. Yes.">>

The brow procedure is similar — a gel application, warmth, the cellular stirring. This time Dr. Maren shapes with their fingers, tracing the new arch with a precision that feels more like sculpture than medicine. They murmur coordinates to themselves — angles, millimeters, the geometry of a face being redrawn in real time.

<<dr_maren "Feathered. Soft, natural-looking. The arch opens the eye area and raises the apparent brow line, reducing the visual weight of the brow ridge. The effect is... lightening. The face becomes lighter. More open. More inviting.">>

Thirty minutes. The warmth fades. Dr. Maren holds the mirror up again.

The brows are — they're subtle. The change from my previous brows — thick, masculine, low-set, slightly unruly — to the Feathered sculpt is like the difference between a rough sketch and a clean line drawing. The new brows are thinner, shaped, with a soft feminine arch that lifts the eye area and creates the impression of more space between the eyes and the hairline. My forehead looks different. Taller. Smoother. The brow ridge, which Dr. Maren identified as a "masculine marker," is visually reduced — not gone, but softened, the way turning down the bass changes a song without removing any notes.

Combined with the lashes, the effect is—

I look at my face in the mirror and I feel a vertigo that has nothing to do with height or motion. The face is mine. I can trace every feature back to the face I've been avoiding in my bathroom mirror for eight months. But the sum is different from the parts. The lashes plus the brows plus the accumulated effect of two small changes equals something new — something that lives in the space between what I was and what I might become, and looking at it is like looking through a window into a room I didn't know my house contained.

<<dr_maren "The brows will continue to refine over the next two to three hours as the cells complete their restructuring. By this evening, the arch will be fully defined.">>

<<eli "It looks... I look...">>

<<dr_maren "You look like yourself. Just... more so.">>

They say it with the ghost of a smile — the first expression I've seen on their face that isn't clinical neutrality. It's the smile of an artist looking at a work in progress. Satisfaction. Potential recognized.

---

Lina handles the wig fitting. She leads me to a small side room — more boutique than clinic, with a lit vanity mirror and a wall of wig forms displaying a dozen styles, each one looking more like real hair than anything I've seen in a store.

<<lina "Dr. Voss mentioned you're streaming voice-only currently? Planning to add a camera element eventually?">>

<<eli "Maybe. I'm not sure yet.">>

<<lina "Well, when you are, this will help.">>

She pulls a wig from the wall — medium-length, brownish-auburn, soft waves, the kind of hair that looks like it belongs to a woman who uses conditioner and knows what a hair mask is. She sets it on the counter in front of me.

<<lina "This is our StreamLine series. Designed specifically for camera use — the fiber responds to light the way biological hair does, so it doesn't have that wig 'sheen' that cheap synthetics get under LED or ring light. Adjustable cap, invisible lace front, blends with your natural hairline.">>

She helps me put it on. The process is more involved than I expected — she tucks my real hair, adjusts the cap, aligns the lace front, uses some kind of adhesive along the edges. Her hands are efficient, impersonal, warm. She smells like vanilla and something clean, like freshly laundered linen.

<<lina "There.">>

She turns me toward the mirror.

I don't recognize myself.

That's not — that's not accurate. I *recognize* myself. The eyes are mine. The nose is mine. The mouth is mine. But the frame has changed. The lashes, the brows, the hair — three modifications, two of them free — and the face in the mirror looks like a cousin. A sister. A version of me from a parallel universe where the genetic dice rolled differently, where the X chromosome doubled, where the person I might have been got to exist instead of the person I am.

The wig falls in soft waves to my shoulders. The brownish-auburn color works with my skin tone — the pale complexion that looked sickly with my greasy brown hair looks *delicate* with this, like the pallor is intentional, porcelain rather than neglected. The lashes frame the eyes. The brows lift the face. The hair completes the composition.

I look androgynous. Not female — not yet, not with the jaw and the nose and the flat chest and the Adam's apple — but not definitively male either. I look like a question the viewer's brain has to answer, and the answer depends on context. In the right lighting, with the right angle, from the nose down or the eyes up—

From the nose down.

An idea forms. Not a full plan — a fragment, a shard, the edge of something. Camera from the nose down. The wig. The voice modulator. The lashes and the brows framing the visible portion of the face. Enough to suggest. Enough to imply. Enough to let the viewer's brain answer the question the way it wants to.

<<lina "The wig comes with a care kit — storage form, fiber shampoo, a styling guide. It's yours. A welcome gift from RegenaForm.">>

<<eli "Thank you.">>

<<lina "Of course. We'll see you soon, Ellie.">>

She says it with the certainty of a person who knows the difference between "goodbye" and "see you soon," who understands that the free eyelashes and the free wig are not charity but investment, and that the investment will pay off because it always does, because the door only opens one way.

I leave the clinic with a bag containing the wig and the care kit and the receipt for the brow sculpt — 300 $, seven-day payment window — and I walk four blocks back to my apartment in the January cold with my new lashes catching snowflakes I didn't notice starting and my new brows still subtly reshaping beneath the skin and the wig in a bag that swings from my hand like a prop from a life I'm rehearsing for.

The splinter in my chest is there. I can feel it — small, sharp, positioned in the exact place where the warm pulse of Kira's red heart lives. Two sensations occupying the same space: the guilt and the hope, the lie and the love, the secret and the girl who doesn't know it exists.

I check my phone. Two hours until Kira picks me up.

Two hours to hide everything.
