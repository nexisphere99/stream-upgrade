# DAY 3 — "The Door" (Part 2: RegenaForm / Marc) — STANDARD ROUTE

---

Suite 12. Second floor. A frosted glass door with a chrome double helix etched into it, catching the hallway's fluorescent light and turning it into something elegant. No signage. No description. Just the logo and the suite number and the quiet implication that if you're here, you already know what this is.

I push the door open.

The waiting area is designed to make you forget you're in a building on Meridian Ave that also houses an accountant and a bail bondsman. Hardwood floors, polished. Two dove-grey couches with angular precision. A glass coffee table with a single orchid and a stack of lookbooks — glossy, heavy-stock portfolios of before-and-after transformations that sit between the orchid and a small bowl of individually wrapped mints like an art exhibition catalog at the world's most unsettling gallery. The lighting is warm, recessed, the kind that makes everyone look better, which is either thoughtful design or a cruel preview of services available.

And then there's the receptionist.

Lina.

I've seen attractive people before. I live in a city. I browse the internet. I have functioning eyes. But Lina exists at a frequency of beauty that makes the word "attractive" feel like bringing a butter knife to a sword fight. She's standing behind the reception desk in a fitted dove-grey dress with the chrome helix embroidered on the chest, and everything about her — the honey-toned skin, the doe-shaped amber-gold eyes, the caramel-highlighted waves, the lips in a permanent, precise smile — reads as an answer to a question nobody asked. What does "perfect" look like? This. Apparently.

And the thing that gets me — the thing that hooks into my brain and won't let go — is that I can't tell what she is. Ethnically, genetically, categorically. She could be anything. Everything. The universality of her beauty is its own uncanny valley — too balanced, too symmetrical, too carefully composed, like a face designed by a committee whose only mandate was "no flaws."

She's a walking advertisement. RegenaForm's best pitch isn't on the website or in the lookbooks. It's standing behind the desk, smiling, being impossible.

<<lina "Welcome to RegenaForm! You must be Ellie.">>

The voice is melodic, warm, modulated. Every word placed like a brushstroke.

<<eli "Yeah. Hi.">>

My voice sounds like gravel in comparison. Male, flat, the voice that earned $3.50. Standing in front of Lina, I'm acutely aware of every masculine thing about myself — the jaw, the Adam's apple, the broad knuckles, the shoes, the posture. I'm a rough sketch standing in front of a finished painting.

<<lina "Wonderful. Dr. Voss will be right with you. Can I get you anything? Water, tea, sparkling?">>

<<eli "No, I'm good. Thanks.">>

<<lina "Of course. Please make yourself comfortable.">>

I sit on the grey couch. It's beautiful and rigid. I pick up a lookbook and flip through it — transformation after transformation, each spread a before-and-after that challenges the concept of identity. A jaw goes from square to soft. A brow ridge flattens. Skin clears from acne-scarred to porcelain. Each page is a person becoming a different person, or the same person becoming more of themselves, depending on which story you want to tell.

I'm on a spread showing a full facial feminization — a face I would have read as unmistakably male on the left page and unmistakably female on the right — when the back door opens.

Dr. Maren Voss.

Tall. Lean. Mid-thirties. A face that refuses categorization — sharp cheekbones and soft lips, strong brow and arched eyebrows, the kind of face that makes your brain try to assign a gender and fail and try again and fail again and eventually give up. Ash-blonde hair pulled sleekly back. Cool grey-green eyes that look at you the way a scanner looks at a barcode — not judgmentally, not warmly, just *completely*. White lab coat. Black high-collared shirt. The RegenaForm helix pin on the lapel.

<<dr_maren "Ellie. I'm Dr. Maren Voss. Please, come in.">>

I follow them through the door. The consultation room is behind it — warm lighting, two chairs facing each other across a low table, a large wall-mounted screen. More design studio than medical office. The whole clinic is like this — aggressively not-clinical, as if the aesthetic itself is part of the treatment, the first modification being your assumption of what this experience would feel like.

I sit. Dr. Maren sits across from me. The grey-green eyes settle on my face with the attention of someone beginning an inventory.

<<dr_maren "Tell me what brings you here.">>

I've rehearsed this. Multiple versions. The one I deliver is the one that sounds most like a normal person making a normal request:

<<eli "I'm a content creator. Streamer. I want to look better on camera. More... polished. Maybe a little more feminine. Not a full transformation — just enough to be camera-friendly.">>

Dr. Maren listens without expression. The neutral attention of a professional who has heard this before — this specific request, this specific framing, this specific dance between what the client is saying and what the client means.

<<dr_maren "Camera-friendly. Can you elaborate?">>

<<eli "My face doesn't... it doesn't stop people. When they see my thumbnail, they scroll past. I need a face that makes people click.">>

<<dr_maren "You want a face that performs.">>

<<eli "...yeah. A face that performs.">>

<<dr_maren "May I?">>

They gesture toward my face. I nod. They lean forward and take my chin between two fingers — cool, dry, impersonal — and turn my head. Left. Right. Center. Up. Down. The examination is brief and thorough, the way a mechanic examines an engine — checking components, identifying the parts that work and the parts that need replacing.

<<dr_maren "Interesting bone structure underneath the neglect. The jaw has feminine potential — it's not heavily set, just undefined. The brow ridge is moderate. Skin quality is poor but treatable. Eye shape is actually quite good — balanced, proportional. The main masculine reads are the brow, the nose, and the skin texture. Everything else is workable.">>

They release my chin. Turn to the wall screen. A tap on a hidden tablet and my face appears — captured by a camera I didn't notice, displayed in merciless high resolution under clinical lighting. Every pore. Every blemish. Every asymmetry. I look like an evidence photo of my own mediocrity.

A digital overlay appears — architectural lines mapping the bone structure, highlighting the masculine markers in red and the neutral/feminine zones in blue.

<<dr_maren "Here's what I see.">>

They trace the red zones.

<<dr_maren "Brow ridge — moderately heavy. Reduces the vertical space of the eye area, codes masculine. Nose — wide bridge, rounded tip, unremarkable but distinctly male. Jaw — could go either way with treatment. Skin — acne, texture issues, dullness. These are the barriers between your current face and a face that... performs.">>

They tap the screen. The overlay shifts. The red zones morph — the brow softens, the lashes lengthen, the skin smooths. A predictive rendering of the same face with Tier 1 modifications applied. The face on screen is still mine but also not mine — it's the face I'd have if I'd been born with different settings, if the genetic sliders had been positioned two notches to the left on every axis.

<<dr_maren "This is achievable. Tier 1 only. No structural bone work. DNA-based modifications using your own genetic material — no rejection, no scarring, full sensory preservation. The technology activates dormant potential in your existing cells. We're not adding anything foreign. We're unlocking what's already there.">>

Unlocking what's already there. The tagline from the website, delivered in person, with the weight of a doctor's authority and the precision of a sales pitch. I can't tell where the medicine ends and the marketing begins. Maybe there's no line.

<<eli "What would you recommend? To start?">>

<<dr_maren "For a first visit, we typically recommend eyelash enhancement — it's our most popular introductory procedure and we offer it complimentary for new clients.">>

Free. The first one's free.

<<dr_maren "Natural-Long eyelashes. Dense, dark, naturally long. Subtle enough that casual acquaintances won't identify the change consciously, but the effect on camera is dramatic. Lashes frame the eyes. Framed eyes hold attention.">>

<<eli "And beyond that?">>

<<dr_maren "If you'd like to invest in one paid procedure today, the brow sculpt. Feathered — soft, natural-looking, feminine arch. Three hundred Dollars. Opens the eye area, reduces the masculine coding of the brow ridge. Combined with the lashes, the effect is... transformative might be too strong a word. Let's say: significant.">>

300 $. $300. I don't have $300. I have $47 in the bank plus $47 on the ElliePlayz dashboard that hasn't hit my account yet. But the $47 from last night will clear. And if I stream tonight — which I will, which I'm already planning, the stream already taking shape in the back of my mind — the numbers suggest another $40-50 minimum. In two days, the brow sculpt pays for itself.

<<eli "Payment plan?">>

<<dr_maren "We offer credit. Dollars-based. Seven-day payment window. The eyelashes are complimentary. The brow sculpt — 300 $ — billed to your streaming account.">>

<<eli "My streaming account?">>

<<dr_maren "ElliePlayz.">>

I don't ask how they know. I should ask how they know. A doctor at a clinic I found on Google two hours ago just said my fake streaming account name — the one I created last night at midnight, the one with 47 followers, the one that exists in a pocket of the internet so small you'd need a microscope to find it — and I should be alarmed by this. I should feel the hair on my neck stand up and the alarm bells ring and the rational part of my brain should be screaming *how do they know, who told them, what are they tracking*.

But the $47 is on the dashboard and the rendering of my face on the screen looks like a person people would click on and Dr. Maren's grey-green eyes are steady and the word "complimentary" is still hanging in the air like a gift, and I don't ask. I cross the bridge without looking down.

<<eli "Okay. Let's do it. Eyelashes and brow sculpt.">>

<<dr_maren "And for streamers, we also include a complimentary professional wig from our StreamLine series. Camera-optimized. Natural appearance. Consider it a welcome gift.">>

Three modifications. Two free. One on credit. The math of the deal is so favorable it should be suspicious, and it is suspicious, and I take it anyway because the rendering on the screen shows a face that could stream without hiding behind a nose-down crop and the numbers say that face would be worth more than $47 an hour.

<<dr_maren "Excellent. Follow me.">>

---

The procedure room. Reclining chair. Adjustable lights. A tray of instruments that look less like medical tools and more like the contents of a very expensive artist's kit. The screen on the wall shows my face with the target areas highlighted — lashes in blue, brows in green.

I lie back. The chair reclines silently — no wheezing, no hydraulic death rattle, just smooth mechanical precision. The ceiling above is white and featureless. No crack. The absence feels wrong, like a face without features.

<<dr_maren "Eyelash enhancement first. Twenty minutes. You'll feel warmth at the application site. Close your eyes.">>

I close them. A cool gel touches my eyelids. Then warmth — spreading, gradual, not unpleasant. Like sunlight through a window, concentrated on the tissue around my eyes. I can feel activity — not movement, not growth in the way you'd imagine growth, but a stirring. Something cellular being switched on. Dormant potential being activated. The science or the poetry of it — I can't tell which and I'm not sure it matters.

Twenty minutes. I lie there with my eyes closed and I think about the numbers.

$47 per hour × 3 hours per day × 7 days = $987 per week.
$987 per week × 4 weeks = $3,948 per month.

Four thousand dollars a month. Streaming as Ellie. That's rent plus groceries plus utilities plus enough left over to start climbing the transformation tiers, to invest back into the face, to make the face better which makes the numbers higher which makes the face better which makes the numbers higher—

The loop is already running. I can feel it — the feedback circuit, the escalation logic, the same compulsive optimization that Kira described when she talked about her content strategy except pointed inward. At myself. At my face. The product and the marketing are the same thing and the thing they're selling is me and the buyer is the internet and the currency is attention.

<<dr_maren "Eyelashes complete. Open your eyes.">>

I open them. Mirror. My face. The same face, mostly. But the eyes—

The lashes. Long, dark, dense, fanning out from my eyelids like the bristles of a brush dipped in ink. They catch the clinical light and cast micro-shadows on my cheekbones, tiny half-moons of shade that didn't exist twenty minutes ago. The eyes themselves are the same — brown, tired, the dark circles still visible — but the framing has changed. The lashes create a border, a declaration, a reason for a viewer's gaze to pause.

<<dr_maren "Natural-Long. Dense, dark. No maintenance. They'll grow at the same rate as your natural lashes. The length is calibrated for standard webcam distance — one to three feet.">>

Calibrated. For webcam distance. They've thought about this. They've optimized the lash length for the specific use case of being seen through a camera. The product is designed for the market. The market is me.

<<dr_maren "Now the brows. Thirty minutes. Same process.">>

The brow procedure involves more shaping — Dr. Maren's fingers trace the new arch with the precision of someone drawing a line they've drawn a thousand times, mapping the geometry of feminization onto the canvas of my brow ridge. The warmth spreads across my forehead, concentrated at the brow line, and I feel the restructuring — the cells shifting, reorganizing, the thick masculine arch thinning and lifting into something feathered and soft.

Thirty minutes. Mirror again.

The brows have changed. Not dramatically — not yet. Dr. Maren says they'll continue refining for two to three hours, the full shape emerging gradually. But already the difference is visible. The thick, low-set, slightly unruly brows I've had my whole life are thinner now, with a soft arch where there was none, a curve that lifts the eye area upward and creates the impression of more space, more openness, more *approachability*. The combination of lashes and brows makes my forehead look different — less heavy, less masculine, lighter in the way that Dr. Maren described.

I look at my face. The face with the new lashes and the reshaping brows and the same acne and dark circles and thin lips. It's a face in transition — not between genders, not yet, but between states. Between the face that earns $3.50 and the face that might earn more. Between EliPlays and ElliePlayz. Between the person I was yesterday and the person I'm deciding to become.

<<dr_maren "The brows will reach their final shape by this evening. By tomorrow, you won't remember what the previous ones looked like.">>

---

Lina fits the wig. A side room — vanity mirror, lit, a wall of wig forms. She selects one without asking my preference, as if the choice was made before I arrived.

<<lina "StreamLine series. Medium-length, soft waves, warm brunette. The fiber is camera-optimized — responds to light the way biological hair does, no synthetic sheen. The cap is adjustable, the lace front invisible.">>

She puts it on me. Tucks my hair. Adjusts the cap. Aligns the lace. Her hands are efficient and warm and smell like vanilla and something that might be fabric softener or might be the ambient scent of someone who was manufactured to be perfect.

<<lina "There.">>

She turns me to the mirror.

The person looking back at me is—

Not me. Except it is me. Every feature, individually, is mine — the eyes, the nose, the mouth, the skin. But the sum has changed. The wig — warm brunette waves falling to the shoulders, soft and real-looking and catching the vanity light like hair in a shampoo ad — reframes the entire face. The lashes anchor the eyes. The reshaping brows lift the forehead. The hair completes the illusion that isn't quite an illusion because it's built from my own cells, my own genetics, my own "dormant potential" switched on by a clinic four blocks from my apartment.

I don't look female. I look... ambiguous. From certain angles, in certain light, the face could go either way. The jaw is still too angular, the Adam's apple still visible, the skin still rough. But from the nose up — lashes, brows, hair — the read is feminine. From the nose down — jaw, throat, stubble shadow — the read is male. And the nose itself is neutral territory, the DMZ between two interpretations of the same face.

From the nose down. Camera positioned to show from the nose down. Or from the nose up. Different framings for different truths.

<<lina "The wig comes with a care kit. It's yours — our welcome gift. We hope to see you again soon, Ellie.">>

The way she says "soon" has the same certainty as "we'll see you soon" at the end of a first dealer's sample. Not if. When.

<<eli "Thanks.">>

<<lina "Of course. Dr. Voss wanted me to mention — your Dollars credit is active. The brow sculpt balance is due within seven days. And if you'd like to explore Tier 1 further... the full menu is available through our app.">>

An app. Of course there's an app. Of course the bioengineering clinic that makes you prettier has a mobile application for browsing the catalog and scheduling procedures and managing your transformation like a subscription service. Of course.

<<eli "I'll think about it.">>

<<lina "Take your time. The door is always open.">>

The door is always open. And it only opens one way.

---

I leave RegenaForm with a bag containing the wig and the care kit and the knowledge that my face is actively restructuring itself as I walk. The January air hits me — cold, clean, the kind that makes your newly enhanced eyelashes feel longer because the cold air moves against them differently. I can feel them. The lashes. A faint awareness of their presence, like wearing new jewelry you haven't adjusted to yet.

Four blocks home. I walk fast, the bag swinging from my hand, head down, hood up. Nobody looks at me. Nobody has ever looked at me — I'm the guy people scroll past, the face that doesn't register — and right now the invisibility is a gift because I'm walking home from a clinic that changed my face and I need to know if the changes are visible.

I round the corner of my block and—

<<marc "Yo!">>

Marcus. Standing on the sidewalk in front of the laundromat, holding a bag of clean clothes, wearing the oversized charcoal hoodie and basketball shorts despite it being January, because Marcus dresses for the weather he wants, not the weather he has. His beat-up Jordans are untied. The scar on his left eyebrow catches the afternoon light.

<<marc "Where you coming from?">>

My heart rate spikes. The bag from RegenaForm suddenly weighs forty pounds. I shift it behind my leg.

<<eli "Just walking. Needed air.">>

<<marc "You don't go outside.">>

<<eli "I go outside.">>

<<marc "Name the last time.">>

<<eli "...yesterday. Kira and I—">>

<<marc "Before yesterday.">>

<<eli "I went to the convenience store last week.">>

<<marc "For Monster Energy.">>

<<eli "It was outside.">>

He looks at me. The Marcus look — direct, unhurried, the brown eyes that hold you and don't let go. He's scanning my face the way he always does, the quick visual check-in that he'd never admit is a welfare assessment but which is, objectively, a welfare assessment. He does it every time he sees me. Every time, for two years, Marcus has looked at my face and read it like a status report.

<<marc "You look different.">>

My stomach drops. The lashes. The brows. He sees them. He—

<<marc "You do something with your hair?">>

<<eli "Washed it.">>

<<marc "With what, actual shampoo?">>

<<eli "Hotel shampoo. Marc gave it to me.">>

<<marc "I know. I'm Marc.">>

<<eli "Right. Yeah.">>

He squints at me for another second. Then shrugs.

<<marc "Looks better. You should wash it more than once a year.">>

<<eli "Noted.">>

<<marc "Where you headed?">>

<<eli "Home. Streaming stuff.">>

<<marc "The mysterious streaming stuff.">>

<<eli "It's not mysterious. It's just... stuff.">>

<<marc "You and Kira both say that. 'Stuff.' Like it's classified.">>

<<eli "It's not classified. It's boring.">>

<<marc "Nothing about you is boring, bro. Tragic, maybe. Not boring.">>

He adjusts the laundry bag on his shoulder. The conversation is casual — this is how we always talk, in the space between going somewhere and getting there, a running commentary that doesn't ask for depth but allows it. Marcus never forces the serious stuff. He just walks beside it and waits.

<<marc "You talk to Kira today?">>

<<eli "Yeah. Briefly.">>

<<marc "How's that going? After the... thing?">>

The thing. The moment. The "it's hot" and the laugh and the taking it back.

<<eli "Fine. Normal.">>

<<marc "Normal.">>

<<eli "We're friends, Marc.">>

<<marc "Mhm.">>

<<eli "Don't mhm me.">>

<<marc "You called her hot and then said 'just kidding' like someone defusing a bomb they built themselves, and now you're telling me it's 'normal.' That's not normal. That's emotional bomb disposal.">>

<<eli "It was a joke.">>

<<marc "It wasn't.">>

<<eli "Marc—">>

<<marc "I'm just saying. You had the moment. You bailed. And now you're doing 'streaming stuff' on a Thursday afternoon instead of talking to her about it.">>

He's right. He's always right. Marcus has this infuriating quality of being correct about emotional situations he's observing from the outside, like a sports commentator who can see plays developing before the players can. He saw the Kira thing coming. He called it. And he's watching me fumble the aftermath with the same mixture of affection and exasperation that defines our entire friendship.

<<eli "I'll figure it out.">>

<<marc "You're not going to figure it out. You're going to avoid it until it becomes impossible to avoid and then panic. That's your pattern.">>

<<eli "I don't have a pattern.">>

<<marc "You have the most consistent pattern of anyone I know. Step one: identify problem. Step two: acknowledge problem. Step three: do absolutely nothing about problem until problem becomes crisis. Step four: panic.">>

<<eli "That's... uncomfortably accurate.">>

<<marc "I've had two years of data.">>

We stand there on the sidewalk. The January wind cuts between the buildings. My face is changing beneath the skin — the brows still reshaping, the cells still restructuring — and Marcus is two feet away and he doesn't see it. He looked at my face, the face he's known for years, and he noticed the hair and nothing else.

The lashes, the brows — they're invisible to him. Not invisible objectively — they're there, they're physical, they're real — but invisible *socially*. The changes are subtle enough that a person who knows my face reads them as "he washed his hair" rather than "he went to a bioengineering clinic and had his eyelashes modified and his brows restructured."

The test passed. The first test. If Marcus — who has two years of data, who does welfare assessments with his eyes, who notices when I text at 4 AM about penguin knees — doesn't see it, then nobody will.

<<marc "Alright, I gotta drop this off and get to work. ScreenFixx doesn't fix itself.">>

<<eli "That's actually the business model.">>

<<marc "Ha. See you later. And talk to Kira.">>

<<eli "I'll think about it.">>

<<marc "You'll avoid it. But I said my piece.">>

He walks off. The laundry bag bounces against his back. His untied Jordans slap the pavement. I watch him go — my best friend, my welfare assessor, the person who tells me truths I don't want to hear — and I feel the weight of the bag in my hand. The wig. The care kit. The evidence of a choice he doesn't know about, in a bag he didn't ask about, behind the leg he wasn't looking at.

I walk the last block home.

The apartment. The door. The key. The hallway that smells like cleaning products and someone's dinner. The stairs (elevator still sounds like a submarine, still don't trust it). The door to my unit. The lock. The interior.

Same apartment. Same crack. Same everything.

Except me.

I set the bag on the desk. Open it. The wig sits inside, nestled in tissue paper, the brownish-auburn waves catching the overhead light (flicker, flicker, on) like something alive. I touch it. The fiber is soft — softer than my real hair, softer than anything in this apartment, which is a low bar but still.

I place it on the desk beside the monitors. It sits there like a prop waiting for its scene.
