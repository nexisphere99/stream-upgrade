# DAY 3 — "The Door" (Part 3: The Secret Stream) — STANDARD ROUTE

---

8:00 PM. The apartment is dark except for the monitors. I've spent the last three hours in a state I can only describe as preparation — not the productive kind, not the kind that results in a clean desk or an organized scene layout, but the internal kind. The psychological loading screen. The process of deciding to do something you've already decided to do.

The wig is on.

I watched the YouTube tutorial twice, pausing at each step, treating the process like a surgical procedure. Tuck the natural hair — the Marriott-shampoo hair, clean for once — beneath a wig cap I fashioned from a cut-up stocking (the tutorial's recommendation for people who don't own a wig cap, which is most people, which is definitely me). Position the wig. Align the lace front with the natural hairline. Secure with adhesive strips that came in the care kit, clear and thin, designed to be invisible at camera distance.

The wig settles onto my head. The weight is — it's not heavy, exactly, but it's *present*. A constant, gentle pressure around the scalp, like wearing a beanie made of someone else's identity. The brownish-auburn waves fall to my shoulders, and every time I move my head they move with me, swaying, catching light, behaving like hair is supposed to behave. The fiber is extraordinary — it has the same slight irregularity as biological hair, the same way of separating into strands when you touch it, the same play of light and shadow along its length. Lina said "camera-optimized" and she meant it. Under the monitor glow, the wig looks real. Realer than my actual hair, honestly, which says something about my hair or the wig or both.

I check the webcam preview in OBS. The full face — wig, lashes, reshaping brows — appears in the small rectangle, and I study it the way I studied the Twitch browse page yesterday, looking for the thing that makes people click.

The face is... it's getting there. The wig transforms the silhouette — the frame of the face is feminine now, soft waves instead of greasy curtain. The lashes anchor the eyes. The brows, almost fully reshaped now (Dr. Maren said two to three hours and it's been five and the arch is defined, graceful, unmistakably different from what was there before), lift the whole upper face into something lighter and more open. From the nose up, the read is ambiguous-to-feminine. From the nose down, the jaw gives it away — too angular, too wide, the shadow of stubble visible even after I shaved twice in the bathroom (once normally, once with painstaking care, getting the blade into every contour, then moisturizing with the body lotion I found under the sink that expired in 2023 but still seems to work).

So: camera from the nose down. The same framing as last night's idea, except now there's a wig in frame, which adds the visual of hair cascading over the shoulders and creates the impression of a full person just outside the camera's reach. The lips (thin, pale — the weakest element, but passable in warm lighting). The chin (okay). The jaw (the liability, but softened by the hair draping alongside it). The neck (Adam's apple hidden by the high neckline of the dark shirt I'm wearing, chosen specifically for this purpose).

I adjust the desk lamp. Warm, directional light from the left. The effect in the webcam preview is — it's good. The warm light softens the jaw shadow. The wig hair catches the light and glows. The lashes are visible as dark fringes at the top of the frame when I tilt my head down slightly. The overall impression is: a girl, seen from the chin down, in warm lighting, with nice hair and a shy disposition.

I turn on VoxMorph. Route the mic. Test.

I take off the wig. The removal is slower than the application — I peel the adhesive strips carefully, lift the cap, and the wig comes free, and my real hair is underneath, matted and sweaty from being compressed. In the dead webcam preview, the face that appears is the one I was born with — modified, yes, with the lashes and the brows, but fundamentally, structurally, a guy's face. Eli's face. The face worth $3.50.

I set the wig on the care form beside the desk. The brownish-auburn waves settle into stillness. Without it, the lashes and brows look strange — feminine accessories on a masculine frame, like earrings on a scarecrow. The discontinuity is visible and uncomfortable and I look away from the monitor.

I put the phone down. Lie on the mattress. Grey sheets. Thin comforter. The ceiling crack.

The numbers run behind my closed eyes, projected on the dark screen of my eyelids like a ticker:

ElliePlayz — Day 2: $47 (voice only). Day 3: $94 (chin cam). Total: $141 in two sessions. 159 followers. 230 peak viewers.

Two days as Ellie have produced 78% of eight months of Eli. Two streams have nearly matched a career. The math doesn't require a calculator. It requires a decision.

The decision has already been made. It was made when I downloaded VoxMorph. It was made when I typed "ElliePlayz" into the username field. It was made when I walked into RegenaForm and sat in the chair and felt the warmth of cells restructuring on my eyelids. The decision is done. What remains is the rationalization.

So I rationalize.

*It's just business.* The streaming economy rewards a specific input. Kira said so herself. She identified the market inefficiency — female faces generate more revenue — and I'm exploiting it. That's not deception. That's arbitrage. That's capitalism. Every business operates on the principle of giving the customer what they want, and the customer wants a cute girl with a nice voice, and I'm providing that service. The product is real (the entertainment, the gameplay, the personality). The packaging is enhanced (the voice, the wig, the face). Every product has packaging. Nobody accuses Coca-Cola of lying because the can is red.

*It's temporary.* I'll do this until I have enough money to actually invest in my life — a better apartment, a better setup, maybe even a ring light. Then I'll pivot. I'll take the skills I've learned and the audience I've built and I'll figure out a sustainable path. Maybe I'll come clean. Maybe I'll rebrand. Maybe ElliePlayz will become a character, a persona, an openly fictional creation like a VTuber. The lie has an expiration date. It just doesn't have one yet.

*Nobody gets hurt.* The viewers are entertained. They're choosing to be here. They're getting value — laughter, companionship, the parasocial warmth of a cute voice in the dark. The donation is a transaction, not a con. They pay for entertainment. I provide entertainment. The fact that the entertainer isn't who they think she is doesn't change the quality of the product.

*Kira would understand.* If I told her — when I tell her — she'd get it. She described the system. She identified the problem. She'd see that I'm solving it the only way I can. She'd understand because she's smart and pragmatic and she knows the industry and she said, in her own apartment, sitting in her ring-lit chair, *"The biggest reason is this"* and she gestured at her face. She TOLD me the answer. I'm just using it.

The rationalizations stack. Neatly. Efficiently. Like bricks. Each one load-bearing. Each one plausible. Together, they form a wall between what I'm doing and what it means. A wall between Eli and Ellie. A wall between the $94 on the dashboard and the red heart from Kira and the wig on the desk and the crack in the ceiling.

It's just business.

It's temporary.

Nobody gets hurt.

She'd understand.

I close my eyes. The fridge hums. The brows continue their slow, cellular restructuring in the dark. The lashes rest against my cheeks — new, dense, dark, the first physical evidence of a transformation that has no clear endpoint.

I don't dream about Kira tonight.

I dream about a number.

$94.

And the number becomes $200. And the $200 becomes $500. And the $500 becomes a dashboard with four digits and a face that stops scrolls and a chair at a desk with a ring light and cable management and a life that works.

And somewhere in the dream, behind the numbers, the ceiling crack is growing.
