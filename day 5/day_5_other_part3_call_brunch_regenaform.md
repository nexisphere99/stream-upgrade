<<marc "You're buying food. With money. That you earned.">>

<<eli "Don't make it weird.">>

He hangs up. The apartment settles into the silence after a phone call — the specific quiet that comes from a voice departing, the air rearranging itself around the absence.

Brunch. Real brunch. The first meal I've purchased with my own money in — I genuinely cannot calculate how long. Months. The math of poverty isn't tracked in purchases made but in purchases not made, and the column of not-made stretches back to the beginning.

I order from a place called Morning Glory. Egg sandwich — avocado, pickled onion, brioche bun. Seasoned potatoes. A latte, oat milk, large. The total is $23.47 with delivery and tip.

$23.47. Approximately twelve minutes of streaming as Ellie. Twelve minutes of a manufactured voice converted into avocado and a bun that's still warm when it arrives and a latte that froths correctly because the place has an actual espresso machine and not a hot plate.

The knock. The paper bag. The delivery person who doesn't look at my face. I take the bag and close the door and stand in my apartment holding food I bought and the holding itself is the meal — the sustenance isn't the calories but the agency, the proof that the numbers on the dashboard translate into something I can taste.

I sit at the desk. Unwrap. Bite. The brioche yields. The yolk pools. The pickled onion is sharp and bright and the avocado is ripe — actually ripe, not the chalky underripe or the brown overripe that I used to get from the convenience store on the rare occasions I attempted fresh produce. This is the avocado of a person who can afford to pick the right one.

The latte is good. The oat milk froths into something sweet and smooth and I drink it at the desk watching the morning dashboard — $211, 302 followers — and the coffee tastes like proof. Like the first line on a receipt that reads EARNED instead of RECEIVED.

I eat everything. The potatoes. The yolk. The foam at the bottom of the latte cup. I set the empty cup beside the monitor. The Morning Glory logo — a sun rising over a coffee bean — sits there like a trophy. The first of its kind.

$23.47 well spent. The best twelve minutes of streaming I've ever converted.

1:15 PM. Forty-five minutes until RegenaForm.

I shower — quick, bar soap, the hair clean enough from this morning. Black t-shirt. Joggers. Hoodie. The uniform that hasn't caught up with the income.

Four blocks. Twelve minutes. The route I've memorized — past the laundromat, the corner store, the bail bondsman, the door at the end of the second-floor hallway with the chrome helix etched in frosted glass.

The waiting area. The orchid. The rigid couches. Lina behind the desk, amber-gold eyes, the smile that deepens fractionally for returning clients.

<<lina "Ellie! Welcome back.">>

<<eli "Hey, Lina.">>

<<lina "Dr. Voss is prepping. The usual? Matcha?">>

The usual. I have a "usual" at a bioengineering clinic. Four days ago I didn't have a usual anywhere. Now I have a usual at a place that restructures faces using DNA compounds and serves matcha in cups that cost more than my desk chair.

<<eli "Sure. Thanks. Also — I want to settle the brow sculpt balance. The three hundred.">>

<<lina "Of course.">>

She pulls up my profile on the tablet. The interface — clean, clinical, my face in the corner, the procedure history, the outstanding balance.

<<eli "Pay the three hundred now. And put the nose — Slope — on a new seven-day credit.">>

<<lina "Wonderful. Brow sculpt cleared. Nose refinement — five hundred — on seven-day terms. Done.">>

$300 gone. The debt that's been sitting on my chest dissolves. Paid with money I earned. My money. Ellie's money. The distinction blurs and I let it blur because the feeling of paying a debt — of watching a number go from $300 to $0 by my own hand — transcends the identity question. Eli or Ellie, the debt is paid. The person who pays it is the person who earned it and they're the same person and the debt is gone.

<<lina "Dr. Voss is ready.">>

The procedure room. The reclining chair. Dr. Maren standing beside it, lab coat pressed, helix pin catching light, the grey-green eyes already inventorying my face as I walk in.

<<dr_maren "Ellie. The lashes and brows have integrated beautifully. Excellent cellular response.">>

<<eli "Thanks. They feel like mine now.">>

<<dr_maren "They are yours. That's the point. Today — the nose. Slope refinement. We'll narrow the bridge by approximately three millimeters per side, straighten the profile, soften the tip from rounded to gently angled. Forty minutes. Same process. You'll feel warmth.">>

<<eli "And after?">>

<<dr_maren "The nose is one of the primary masculine tells. With the Slope done, the upper two-thirds of your face will read as convincingly feminine in most contexts. On camera, with your current framing, the nose will integrate with the lash and brow work to create a cohesive upper-face composition. The crop line drops. More face. More engagement.">>

More face. More engagement. The doctor speaks in the same vocabulary as the YouTube streamer coaches, the same language as the Twitch algorithm, the clinical and the commercial indistinguishable. Medicine and marketing, the double helix.

I sit. Recline. The crackless white ceiling.

<<dr_maren "Close your eyes.">>

The compound. Cool gel on the bridge, across the tip, along the sides. Then warmth — deeper than before, penetrating the cartilage, the architecture of the nose restructuring millimeter by millimeter. I feel it. Not pain but change. The awareness of something shifting beneath the skin, the cells receiving new instructions, dormant potential switching on.

Forty minutes. I lie there and I think.

I think about Kira. The homework. The text I haven't written. "Actual words about actual feelings." The Venn diagram of what I feel and what I can safely reveal — the tiny overlap in the center where "I like you" and "I'm scared" intersect. I could write that. I could send that. Three sentences. I like you. I'm scared. I don't know how to be both the person you want and the person I'm becoming.

Except the third sentence opens a door I'm not ready to walk through. The person I'm becoming. She'd ask what that means. And the answer is: I'm lying on a table in a clinic having my nose reshaped because I stream as a woman and the money requires a face that matches the voice. That's the actual feeling. That's the real thing under the real thing. And I can't text that between heart emojis.

I think about Marcus. Coming over at five. The direct brown eyes that do welfare assessments. He didn't notice the lashes or the brows — attributed everything to "the Marriott shampoo." But the nose is different. The nose is structural. The bridge narrowing, the tip refining — these are changes that alter the center of the face, the feature that anchors everything else. If he looks at me the way he looks at me — the Marcus scan, the two-year dataset of my face — will the nose register?

Maybe not. Marcus doesn't study faces. Marcus studies feelings. He'll look at me and see "Eli seems better" and the nose will dissolve into the general impression the way the lashes did, the way the brows did, the specific absorbed into the general.

Maybe.

<<dr_maren "Structural work complete. Opening your eyes.">>

Mirror. The nose.

Different. The word lands first, before analysis, before the brain breaks the change into components. Different. The bridge has narrowed — the wide, masculine spread that dominated the center of my face for twenty-two years has contracted, drawing the lines of the nose inward, creating a profile that runs straight instead of bulging. The tip has softened — the rounded, blunt thing that gave the whole feature its masculine stamp has refined into a gentle downward angle, a slope that Dr. Maren's catalog named and the bioactive compound delivered. The asymmetry is gone. Left and right match. The nose is, for the first time in my life, symmetrical.

Combined with the lashes and the brows, the upper face is — crossing a line. Not approaching it, not nearing it, crossing it. The forehead-to-upper-lip region reads as female. Not ambiguously. Not "could go either way." Female. A stranger seeing this portion of my face would classify it without hesitation, without second thought, the way the brain classifies faces in half a second based on the aggregate of features.

The lower third — jaw, lips, chin — remains Eli. The unchanged territory. The sketch beneath the painting. The masculine frame that the crop line hides and the wig can't reach.

But on camera. In the webcam frame. With the wig.

The fitting room. Lina. The wig — my StreamLine, the brownish-auburn waves, the camera-optimized fiber. She puts it on. Tuck, position, align. The practiced hands, the vanilla-and-linen scent.

<<lina "There.">>

Mirror.

The face in the glass is closer to Ellie than it's ever been. The wig frames the composition — warm waves falling past the shoulders, catching the vanity light. The lashes anchor the eyes. The brows arch. And now the nose — the Slope, narrow and straight and gently angled — sits at the center like a keystone, the piece that makes the arch hold, the element that transforms a collection of modifications into a coherent face.

From the eyes to the nose. Two-thirds feminine. The crop line can drop. Tonight, the webcam shows Ellie's eyes AND Ellie's nose and the image that emerges will be close enough to a real girl's face that the viewer's brain won't question it. Won't hesitate. Won't scroll past.

I stare at the mirror for fifteen seconds. The face stares back. It's beautiful in a way that my face has never been — not the word I'd use for the old Eli, not the word anyone would use, but the face in the mirror with the wig and the lashes and the brows and the nose is approaching something that the word "beautiful" is willing to live near. Pretty, maybe. Striking, in certain light. Camera-ready, definitely.

I take off the wig. Hand it to Lina. Eli returns — but a different Eli. An Eli with a straight nose that doesn't belong to the jawline it sits above. A face in two languages.

<<lina "The next logical step would be lip enhancement. Natural-Full. Four hundred. But no rush.">>

The upsell. The seed. I barely hear it. I'm looking at the mirror and thinking about Marcus at five o'clock — the direct eyes, the scan, the question that may or may not come.

<<eli "Thanks, Lina. I'll think about it.">>

<<lina "We're always here.">>

Always. The certainty of a system that knows I'll return.

I leave. Four blocks. January air on the new nose — the sensation different, the cold meeting a different architecture, the narrowed bridge and the refined tip registering temperature in a way the old nose didn't. Everything feels more precise. More defined. Like the nose is receiving higher-resolution data from the air.

3:15 PM. Hour and forty-five until Marcus.

Time to buy food. Real food. For two people.

I stop at the Korean place on the corner — the one I've walked past hundreds of times, the one with the handwritten menu in the window that I used to read the way you read real estate listings in a neighborhood you can't afford. Today I walk in. The bell chimes. The woman behind the counter looks at me and doesn't recognize me as the guy who walks past because I've never been the guy who walks in.

I order. Bulgogi with rice, two portions. Kimchi pancake. Japchae. A side of pickled radish. Two cans of that Korean barley tea I've seen Marcus drink.

The total is $38.20. I pay without flinching. Thirty-eight dollars. Approximately eighteen minutes of Ellie's voice. Eighteen minutes to feed two people something that isn't frozen or microwaved or consumed standing over a counter in the dark.

I carry the bags home. Two bags, heavy, the plastic handles cutting into my fingers. The weight feels good. Substantial. The weight of food you bought for a friend with money you earned from a face you're building.

The apartment. I set the bags on the counter. The Korean food sits beside the burrito wrappers — the old diet and the new one, side by side, the before-and-after of a kitchen that's learning what real food looks like.

I throw away the burrito wrappers. Both of them. The wrapper from yesterday AND the one from three days ago. The ones I've been stepping over. I pick them up and put them in the trash and the counter looks different without them — cleaner, emptier, more like a place where food is prepared rather than endured.

I also throw away the empty Monster cans. Two of them, one on the floor by the desk and one on the counter. Into the trash. The apartment doesn't transform — it's still 280 square feet of managed decline — but the decluttering removes a layer of debris that was making the decline look managed by nobody.

4:45 PM. Marcus in fifteen minutes. The nose has settled further — I check the phone camera. The bridge is sharper now, the definition increasing as Dr. Maren promised. The straight line. The soft slope. The face in the camera is the face Marcus is about to see and the face Marcus is about to see has changed since he last looked at it and the question is whether Marcus looks at faces or feelings and the answer has always been feelings but the nose is in the center of the face and the center is hard to miss.

I sit on the mattress. Wait. The Korean food cooling on the counter, the barley tea sweating in its cans, the apartment slightly cleaner than it's been in months, and a face slightly less Eli than it was yesterday.
