Two knocks. Pause. One more. The Marcus rhythm.

I open the door. He's standing in the hallway — charcoal hoodie, basketball shorts, the beat-up Jordans with the untied laces, a plastic bag in one hand. The scar on his eyebrow catches the hallway fluorescent.

<<marc "I brought the good controller.">>

<<eli "And?">>

<<marc "And cheese puffs. The good controller needs cheese puffs. It's a package deal.">>

He walks in. The scan — the quick, automatic, Marcus-does-it-every-time scan. Mattress: made. Sort of. The sheets are pulled up, which is new. Desk: monitors off, nothing visible. Kitchen: no burrito wrappers. Korean food containers on the counter. His eyebrows lift.

<<marc "...is that bulgogi?">>

<<eli "And japchae. And kimchi pancake.">>

<<marc "You bought Korean food.">>

<<eli "I bought Korean food.">>

<<marc "From the place on the corner.">>

<<eli "The place on the corner.">>

He sets the cheese puffs on the counter beside the bulgogi. The two items sit side by side — the currency of the old friendship and the currency of the new income. He looks at the food and then at me and the look holds for a beat longer than the usual scan.

<<marc "Alright, who are you and what have you done with Eli Vance?">>

<<eli "I told you. The streaming stuff is working.">>

<<marc "The streaming stuff bought a forty-dollar Korean dinner.">>

<<eli "Thirty-eight twenty.">>

<<marc "The specificity of that number tells me you've been doing math about it.">>

<<eli "I've been doing math about everything lately.">>

He nods. Doesn't push. Opens the bulgogi container. The smell fills the apartment — soy sauce and sesame and grilled beef and caramelized onion, a scent so far removed from the microwave-burrito baseline that it might as well be from a different country, which technically it is.

<<marc "This smells incredible. Also your apartment is cleaner.">>

<<eli "I threw away the burrito wrappers.">>

<<marc "Both of them?">>

<<eli "Both.">>

<<marc "And the Monster cans?">>

<<eli "Gone.">>

<<marc "Eli. You cleaned and bought food. In the same day.">>

<<eli "Don't make it a whole thing.">>

<<marc "It IS a whole thing. Two weeks ago you were eating frozen burritos in the dark and now there's japchae on the counter and the floor is visible. That's a whole thing.">>

He looks at me again. The second look. This one is different from the entrance scan — it's focused, deliberate, the Marcus look that I've seen a hundred times and that means something's registering. His brown eyes move across my face. Forehead. Eyes. Nose. Jaw. Back to the eyes. Back to the nose.

<<marc "You look different.">>

The sentence. The same sentence he said on the sidewalk two days ago. But this time it carries a different weight because this time the different is more different — the nose has been in the settling process for three hours and the definition has sharpened and the bridge is noticeably narrower and the face he's looking at is the face of a person who went to a clinic this afternoon and had the center of his face restructured.

<<eli "Do I?">>

<<marc "Your face looks... I don't know. Sharper? Something around the middle.">>

My pulse spikes. Around the middle. He's looking at the nose. He doesn't know that he's looking at a nose that was reshaped three hours ago by a DNA-based bioengineering compound in a clinic four blocks away, but his eyes are finding it, the way his eyes always find the thing that's changed, the welfare assessment running its diagnostics and flagging an anomaly.

<<eli "I've been sleeping better. Eating better. Probably just looks different because I'm not running on Monster Energy and despair.">>

<<marc "Hmm.">>

One syllable. Not Kira's "hmm" — Kira's "hmm" is analytical, a filing system at work. Marcus's "hmm" is more like a parking space — he's putting the observation somewhere temporarily, not investigating it, not pressing it, just acknowledging that something registered and choosing to circle back or not.

He chooses not.

<<marc "Well, whatever it is, keep doing it. You look healthy. I haven't been able to say that in a while.">>

<<eli "Thanks, Marc.">>

<<marc "Don't thank me. Feed me. Where are the plates?">>

<<eli "I don't have plates.">>

<<marc "...you don't have plates.">>

<<eli "I have a bowl. One bowl.">>

<<marc "Eli.">>

<<eli "And paper towels.">>

<<marc "We're eating bulgogi off paper towels.">>

<<eli "Welcome to my home.">>

We eat off paper towels and the one bowl (Marcus gets the bowl because he's a guest and because the bulgogi in a bowl is an experience he deserves and I deserve paper towels because I'm a person who doesn't own plates). The food is good — the kind of good that announces itself with the first bite and doesn't need to prove anything after. The beef is tender. The japchae is sweet and savory and slippery in a way that makes eating it with fingers a gamble. The kimchi pancake is crispy at the edges and soft in the center and Marcus eats three-quarters of it before I get to my second piece, which is fair because he's 6'1" and I'm a person who's been eating frozen food for eight months and doesn't have the stomach capacity for real portions yet.

<<marc "This is really good.">>

<<eli "Yeah?">>

<<marc "Yeah, man. Like actually good. Not 'good for a person who eats burritos' good. Actually good.">>

<<eli "I'm upgrading.">>

<<marc "To what?">>

<<eli "To a person who eats food that isn't cylindrical.">>

<<marc "Bold move. Next you'll buy furniture.">>

<<eli "Let's not get ahead of ourselves.">>

We clean up — paper towels into the trash, the one bowl rinsed (I rinse it; Marcus watches me rinse it with the expression of a nature documentarian observing a rare behavior in the wild). The barley tea is cold and clean and Marcus drinks his in four swallows and crushes the can on the counter because Marcus treats all beverages like challenges.

We move to the mattress. The PS4 boots. Rift Walkers loads. Marcus has the good controller — the one he brought, the one that responds correctly, the one that doesn't have a sticky R2. I have the thrift-store replacement. The hierarchy is established.

<<marc "Floor 17 tonight.">>

<<eli "Floor 17.">>

<<marc "We've been saying that for months.">>

<<eli "Tonight it happens.">>

<<marc "Your confidence is new and suspicious.">>

<<eli "Everything about me is new and suspicious.">>

He laughs. The dungeon generates. We play.

The rhythm is the same. The yelling. The panic. The teamwork that functions through insults.

<<eli "BEHIND YOU —">>

<<marc "I know! I hear him!">>

<<eli "You don't hear him, you're DYING —">>

<<marc "I'm not dying I'm strategically retreating —">>

<<eli "INTO A WALL?">>

<<marc "The wall was strategically placed!">>

We die on Floor 14. Run it again. Floor 16. The record, tied again. The Floor 16 boss with six arms does its chain attack and Marcus dodges five of them and the sixth catches his ankle and the ragdoll physics send his character cartwheeling across the arena and I try to revive him and the boss turns and one-shots me while I'm in the revive animation.

<<marc "Floor 16. Again.">>

<<eli "We're cursed.">>

<<marc "We're not cursed. We're bad.">>

<<eli "We're consistently bad. That's a kind of skill.">>

<<marc "The skill of reliable mediocrity.">>

We run it a third time. Floor 11. Worse. The game doesn't care about our ambitions. The game is a system that rewards specific inputs — skill, timing, coordination — and our inputs are inconsistent in a way that no amount of enthusiasm can fix.

I think about the other system. The one that rewards Ellie's inputs. That system responds to different variables — face, voice, performance — and those variables I can control, optimize, purchase. The streaming system is hackable. Rift Walkers isn't.

Between runs, during the loading screen, the conversation shifts.

<<marc "So for real though. The money thing. How much are we talking?">>

He says it casually, the way he says all the important things — dropped into the loading-screen silence like a coin into a well, the depth measured by how long the silence lasts before the splash.

<<eli "What do you mean?">>

<<marc "The streaming. You bought a forty-dollar dinner. You're talking about buying furniture. How much money are you actually making?">>

<<eli "I told you. The numbers are better.">>

<<marc "'Better' is a word. I'm asking for a number.">>

I look at the loading screen. The dungeon generating. The corridors and rooms assembling from code, random and procedural, a world being built from rules.

<<eli "A few hundred. This week.">>

<<marc "A few hundred.">>

<<eli "Yeah.">>

<<marc "In a week.">>

<<eli "In a week. From streaming.">>

<<marc "On EliPlays.">>

The question is specific. The question names the account. The pause before my answer is a half-second too long and Marcus may or may not notice because the loading screen ends and the dungeon generates and the moment passes into the noise of gameplay.

<<eli "From streaming in general. I've been trying different platforms, different approaches.">>

<<marc "Different approaches.">>

<<eli "Yeah. Format stuff. Content strategy.">>

<<marc "A few hundred from content strategy.">>

<<eli "Marc, can we just play?">>

He looks at me. The look. Not hostile, not suspicious in the aggressive sense, but attentive in the Marcus sense — the sense that says I see the outline of a thing you're not showing me and I'm deciding whether to draw the rest of it or wait for you to fill it in.

<<marc "Okay. But Eli — whatever the 'different approaches' are, just... be careful. A few hundred a week is good money. Good money from the internet has a way of coming with conditions.">>

<<eli "What does that mean?">>

<<marc "It means the internet pays for things and the things it pays for aren't always the things you want to sell.">>

The sentence lands like a dropped weight. He doesn't know about Ellie. He doesn't know about the voice modulator or the wig or the clinic. But Marcus has the instinct of a person who's watched people sell pieces of themselves — at ScreenFixx, the boss who sold his integrity for a franchise fee; in the neighborhood, the guys who sold their time for gig apps that paid less than minimum wage and called it "flexibility." Marcus knows what it looks like when a person starts converting parts of their life into revenue and the conversion rate favors the buyer.

<<eli "I'm being careful.">>

<<marc "Good.">>

He turns back to the screen. The dungeon waits. We play.

Floor 12. Floor 9. Floor 15. Close but not close enough.

<<marc "One more run?">>

<<eli "One more.">>

Floor 17.

The number appears on screen and we both go quiet. The walls of the seventeenth floor are different — darker, the ambient sound lower, the monster types new. We've never been here. In two years of playing this game, in dozens of sessions on my mattress and his couch and the floor of his apartment, we've never seen Floor 17.

<<marc "Bro.">>

<<eli "I see it.">>

<<marc "Floor 17.">>

<<eli "New record.">>

We play the floor in near-silence, the banter suspended, the stakes too high for jokes. I play support — healer class, hanging back, keeping Marcus alive while he draws aggro. The synergy works. For four minutes, we function like a team that actually communicates, the button presses synchronized, the timing aligned.

The Floor 17 boss appears. It has eight arms. Two more than the Floor 16 boss.

<<marc "Eight arms.">>

<<eli "I see the eight arms.">>

<<marc "That's two more arms than our nemesis.">>

<<eli "Marc, focus.">>

We fight. We actually fight well — Marcus dodging, me healing, the boss losing health in increments, the bar dropping from full to three-quarters to half. We're going to do it. We're actually going to beat Floor 17.

The boss does an attack we've never seen. A ground slam that sends a shockwave through the entire arena. Marcus jumps it. I don't.

My character dies. Marcus is alone. Boss at 30% health. One player. Eight arms.

He lasts forty-seven seconds. Gets the boss to 12%. Then an arm catches him mid-dodge and the ragdoll sends him into a wall and the health bar empties.

FLOOR 17 — FAILED

<<marc "Twelve percent.">>

<<eli "Twelve percent.">>

<<marc "We got the boss to twelve percent.">>

<<eli "New record. Floor 17. Boss at 12%.">>

<<marc "Next time.">>

<<eli "Next time.">>

He says it with the conviction of a man who has been saying "next time" for two years and who will keep saying it because the saying is the point. The game is the excuse. The sitting beside each other on a mattress in a studio apartment is the game.

8:30 PM. Marcus stands. Stretches. The spine-crack. The sound that makes me wince every time.

<<marc "Gotta go. Early shift tomorrow — the owner's having Devon 'shadow' me, which means I do two people's jobs while Devon watches and drops things.">>

<<eli "Good luck.">>

<<marc "Luck doesn't cover Devon. I need insurance.">>

He picks up the good controller. Tucks it into the hoodie pocket. Marcus carries the good controller the way a knight carries a sword — personally, everywhere, never entrusted to lesser hands.

At the door, he stops. Turns.

<<marc "The food was good, Eli.">>

<<eli "Thanks.">>

<<marc "No. Like — the gesture. Buying food. Having it here when I arrived. That's... that's not something you used to do.">>

<<eli "I know.">>

<<marc "Whatever's changing — whatever the 'approaches' are — I'm glad. You seem better. Like, actually better. Not the 'I'm fine' better. Real better.">>

<<eli "I am better.">>

<<marc "Good.">>

A beat. The hallway behind him, fluorescent, institutional. The elevator making its submarine groan behind the closed doors.

<<marc "And Eli? The Kira thing. The homework. Have you texted her?">>

<<eli "Not yet.">>

<<marc "Not yet.">>

<<eli "I'm working on it.">>

<<marc "You've been working on it for five days.">>

<<eli "Some homework takes longer.">>

<<marc "Some homework takes longer because the student is avoiding it.">>

<<eli "Marc —">>

<<marc "Text her. Tonight. Before you stream or do whatever else you do at midnight. Text her something real. She deserves real.">>

<<eli "I know.">>

<<marc "Do you? Because you keep saying 'I know' and she keeps waiting and the gap between those two things is getting wider.">>

He says it without anger. With the flat, caring precision of a person who loves his friend and is watching his friend do the thing his friend always does — identify the problem, acknowledge the problem, do nothing about the problem until the problem becomes a crisis.

<<marc "Later.">>

<<eli "Later.">>

The door closes. Footsteps. Stairwell. Gone.

The apartment settles. The bulgogi smell lingers. The cheese puffs sit unopened on the counter — Marcus forgot them, or left them deliberately, the distinction irrelevant because the result is the same: a bag of cheese puffs as a standing invitation to continue being friends.

I sit at the desk. The monitors are dark. The closet is closed, the wig inside. The VoxMorph icon is in the taskbar, minimized. The dual reality — Eli's apartment, Ellie's tools — sharing the same 280 square feet.

Marcus said text her. Tonight.

I pick up the phone. Open Kira's thread.

<<kira "morning. did you sleep?">>

<<kira "also: the homework is not optional. actual words. actual feelings. whenever you're ready.">>

The messages from this morning. My replies — minimal, evasive, the emotional equivalent of putting a "back in 5 minutes" sign on a door that's been closed for years.

I type. Delete. Type. Delete. The cursor blinks.

<<eli "hey. it's me. the homework.">>

<<eli "i like you. you know that. i said it in the car and i meant it and i haven't unsaid it and i won't.">>

<<eli "i'm scared of a lot of things right now. some of them i can't explain yet. not because i don't want to but because i don't have the words shaped right. i'm working on it.">>

<<eli "the part i can explain: you matter to me. more than the streaming stuff. more than the numbers. more than anything that's changing. you're the fixed point.">>

<<eli "that's the real thing. that's the actual feeling. i'm sorry it took five days.">>

I read it. Five messages. Real words. Actual feelings. Not all of them — not the Ellie part, not the clinic part, not the voice modulator or the wig. But the Kira part. The part about her. The part that's true regardless of everything else.

I send it. Five messages in rapid succession, the way you jump off a diving board — all at once, no pausing on individual rungs.

Three minutes pass. The longest three minutes of my life, longer than the RegenaForm procedures, longer than the loading screens, longer than the forty-five seconds Marcus survived alone against the Floor 17 boss.

<<kira "eli.">>

<<kira "that wasn't so hard, was it?">>

<<kira "thank you. for the real thing.">>

<<kira "i like you too. i've liked you since before you knew how to say it. you're infuriating and you're worth the wait.">>

<<kira "the parts you can't explain yet — i'll be here when you can. no rush. but no more thumbs-up emojis. that's the deal. ❤️">>

Red heart. The real one.

I send one back. For the first time since Day 1.

<<eli "❤️">>

It costs me something to send it. Not money — something else. Something in the economy of honesty, where every red heart I send while hiding a secret is a debt that accrues interest. But the heart is real even if the context is incomplete. I love her. That's not a lie. Everything around it might be, but the heart itself is genuine.

I put the phone down. The homework is done. Incomplete — the other half of the truth still boxed, still sealed, still waiting for a day I keep pushing forward. But the Kira part is submitted. And she accepted it.

9:30 PM. Stream time.
