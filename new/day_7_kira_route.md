I wake up to the smell of argan oil.

It takes me three full seconds to understand what's happening — the brain rebooting from sleep, the sensory input arriving before the contextual framework — and then I realize: it's my hair. My hair smells like something. Not grease. Not nothing. Not the flat chemical ghost of 3-in-1 body wash that's been my scalp's dominant fragrance for the past however many months. It smells like the inside of a nice store. Like Kira's drugstore aisle. Like twenty-two dollars and forty-seven cents of self-improvement.

I lie on the mattress and I turn my head left, then right, just to feel the strands move against the pillowcase. They move. Individually. Separately. Like hair is supposed to move and hasn't, in my case, since I stopped caring about hair, which was approximately the same time I stopped caring about everything else, which was approximately always.

The ceiling crack is there. Hairline fracture from light fixture to window. Unchanged. Unchangeable. The only constant in an apartment that now contains plates and shampoo and the ghost of a girl's bare breasts in afternoon light and the ghost of her hand and the ghost of a word I said in a grocery store that I'm not sure I can take back or want to.

Phone. 8:39 AM. Early again. The body adjusting to a rhythm it didn't choose — waking for streams, sleeping after streams, the circadian clock syncing to the algorithm's preferences rather than the human's. But there's no alarm. No obligation. Just the argan oil and the morning light through the bent blinds and the particular quiet of a room that's becoming, incrementally, less empty.

A bird — the same bird that's been singing outside the window since spring, a sparrow or a finch or something with wings and an opinion about daylight.

I get up. Bathroom. Mirror. The face.

The face is becoming a daily event — not a check but a reckoning, a calibration, the way a pilot reads instruments before takeoff. What's changed. What hasn't. What's next. The lashes frame the eyes — dense, dark, naturally long. The brows lift the forehead — soft, feathered, that slight arch that reads as curiosity. The nose sits in the center like a declaration — straight, sloped, the bridge narrow and clean, the tip gentle. Three modifications. Three departures from the face I was born with. And below them, unchanged: the flat undefined jawline, the thin pale lips, the acne on the forehead that the blemish purge hasn't touched because I haven't booked the blemish purge because the blemish purge is $200 and the math hasn't allowed it yet.

The upper face is her. The lower face is me. The boundary line runs roughly at the cheekbones — the cheekbones that are still flat, still undefined, still the masculine default. The face needs them. I know it the way I know the crack in the ceiling is there without looking — a gap in the architecture, a room in the cathedral that hasn't been built yet, a note missing from the chord.

Cheekbones. And ears. The next step.

I brush my teeth with the new toothbrush — firm bristles, blue handle, the most deliberate toothbrush purchase in human history, made in the checkout line of a grocery store while Kira was buying cat food. The outfit of a person whose wardrobe is a rotation and whose rotation is three deep and whose ambitions for fashion begin and end with "covers the body."

Stream first. Talk to Kira about the next modification. Then whatever comes after.
