# PROLOGUE — "BEFORE THE STREAM"

---

The year is 2027, and the machines took the jobs before anyone figured out what comes next.

It didn't happen the way the movies promised — no chrome skeletons marching through cities, no single moment where the switch flipped and humanity became obsolete. It happened the way everything happens in capitalism: gradually, then all at once. A chatbot replaced the call center. An algorithm replaced the paralegal. A vision model replaced the radiologist, the quality inspector, the truck driver, the copywriter, the graphic designer, the junior accountant, the data entry clerk, the customer service rep, the translator, the tutor, the social media manager, the content moderator, the warehouse picker, the fast-food line cook. One by one, then ten by ten, then by the thousands — positions dissolving like sugar in hot water, there one quarter and gone the next, replaced by subscriptions to software that didn't need bathroom breaks or health insurance or the will to live.

By 2026, the unemployment numbers had stopped meaning anything because the government kept redefining "employed" to include gig work that paid less than the electricity it required. The official rate said 7%. The real number — the one that counted people who'd stopped looking, people working four gig apps simultaneously to make rent, people whose "job" was selling plasma and filling out surveys for fractions of a cent — was closer to 25%. A quarter of the working-age population, untethered, drifting through an economy that had automated their purpose and offered nothing in return except a login screen and a suggestion to "build your personal brand."

So they did.

The streaming economy exploded not because people wanted to be entertainers but because there was nothing else left. Twitch, YouTube, TikTok, a dozen platforms that rose and fell like empires in fast-forward — together they absorbed the displaced millions the way a sponge absorbs a flood: messily, inadequately, with most of the water ending up on the floor. Every laid-off barista became a Just Chatting streamer. Every automated-out office worker became a gaming channel. Every college graduate who discovered that their $80,000 degree qualified them for exactly zero positions that still existed sat down in front of a webcam and said "hey guys, welcome to the stream" and hoped — with the quiet, animal desperation of a species watching its habitat shrink — that someone would watch.

Someone rarely did.

The streaming economy operates on the same distribution as a casino: a handful of jackpots subsidized by millions of empty pockets. The top 1% of streamers capture 90% of the revenue. The rest stream into the void, burning hours and electricity and hope, performing for audiences of bots and ghosts and one persistent troll who shows up every day to remind them they're failing. The barrier to entry is zero — a laptop and a Wi-Fi connection — which means the competition is infinite, which means the browse page is a graveyard of thumbnail faces scrolling past at terminal velocity, each one a person who bet their future on being interesting enough to stop the scroll.

The algorithm decides who lives and who dies. Not literally — not yet — but in the economic sense that matters. The recommendation engine, the browse page, the For You feed: these are the gods of the new economy, and they are neither kind nor fair. They reward engagement. They reward retention. They reward, above all else, the specific neurological response that a human face triggers in a human brain — the click, the pause, the stay. And some faces trigger it more than others.

The data is clear, uncomfortable, and universally understood by anyone who's spent a week trying to grow a channel: attractive women outperform everyone. Not because they're better at games or funnier or smarter — some are, some aren't, the distribution is normal — but because the audience that funds the streaming economy is predominantly young, predominantly male, and predominantly lonely (lonelier now, in the post-automation quiet, with fewer workplaces to meet people and fewer reasons to leave the apartment), and the algorithm has learned to feed that loneliness with faces it can't look away from. The gender gap in streaming isn't a whisper. It's a scream. A female streamer with 500 viewers will, on average, earn three to five times the donations of a male streamer with the same count. The thumbnail click-through rate for a female face is 40% higher. The subscription retention is double.

Everyone knows this. Nobody says it out loud. The industry pretends the meritocracy is real — *be consistent, engage with chat, find your niche* — because the alternative is admitting that the single most important variable in a streaming career is the genetic lottery. And you can't put that in a YouTube thumbnail.

Or you couldn't. Until RegenaForm.

---

RegenaForm appeared two years ago, quietly, the way all dangerous things appear — a website, a waiting list, a whisper network among streamers and influencers and the body-conscious edges of the internet. No Super Bowl ad. No billboard campaign. Just a chrome double-helix logo and a six-word tagline that embedded itself in the collective consciousness like a splinter:

*We don't change you. We unlock you.*

The technology behind RegenaForm is proprietary, patented, and classified at a level that makes pharmaceutical companies look transparent. What's public is this: they use the client's own DNA as a template. Every modification — every reshaped bone, every restructured tissue, every new configuration of flesh and nerve — is grown from the client's genetic material, activated by a bioactive compound that switches on dormant developmental pathways in existing cells. The body doesn't reject the changes because the changes *are* the body. There's nothing foreign. Nothing synthetic. Nothing that a blood test or a biopsy would flag as artificial.

The results are immediate. The recovery is hours, not weeks. There are no scars. No suture lines. No telltale marks of any kind — not a stitch, not a seam, not a single visible trace that anything was done at all. A jawline can be reshaped in forty minutes. Cheekbones restructured over a lunch break. Skin cleared, smoothed, re-toned to any shade on the human spectrum in the time it takes to watch a movie. Eyelashes grow from the same follicles they always lived in — just longer, darker, denser, as if the body finally remembered what it was supposed to look like all along.

And that's just the face.

RegenaForm's catalog extends from the cosmetic to the radical, with nothing off the table and no procedure considered too extreme. Voices can be modulated organically — not through software but through actual restructuring of the vocal cords, the larynx, the resonance chambers of the throat and sinuses, producing a voice so authentically different that a spectrograph can't tell it from one that was born, not built. Body contour — shoulders narrowed, waists cinched, hips widened, ribcages tapered, fat redistributed along any line the client desires — achieved through skeletal and soft tissue modification that leaves the skin unblemished, the body unmarked, as though the bones had always been this shape.

Breasts can be grown. Not implanted — *grown*. From the client's own mammary tissue, activated and expanded, producing breasts in any size from barely-there to physics-defying. Any shape: teardrop, round, perky, natural. Any sensitivity level, from standard to hyper-responsive, the nerve density calibrated like a dial. Fully functional. Full sensation. Indistinguishable from natal development by any examination — mammogram, biopsy, ultrasound — because they *are* natal development, triggered decades after the original window closed.

Buttocks reshaped. Hips flared. Waists narrowed to proportions that corsets couldn't achieve. Every modification seamless, scarless, grown from within. The body rebuilds itself around the new blueprint, and the new blueprint leaves no evidence of the old one.

And at the far end of the catalog, behind a consultation wall and a financial threshold that filters out everyone except the desperate and the committed: full gender reassignment. Complete. Functional. Unmarked. A vulva built from the client's own cells — labia, clitoris, vaginal canal — self-lubricating, fully sensate, connected to a nervous system that was rewired to receive its signals as pleasure, as sensation, as the authentic experience of a body that matches its configuration. A uterus. Ovaries. Fallopian tubes. The complete architecture of a female reproductive system, grown inside a body that was born male, operating on hormones that the body now produces naturally because RegenaForm didn't just add parts — it rewrote the endocrine system from the ground up.

No surgeon's hands. No operating theatre. No months of recovery, no dilations, no compromises. Just a reclining chair, a cool gel, a warmth that spreads, and a body that wakes up different. Complete. Unmarked. As though it had always been this way.

The process can go in any direction. Male to female. Female to male. Any combination. Any configuration — including ones that don't exist on any natural spectrum. Dual configurations. Switchable anatomy. Bodies that toggle between states on command. RegenaForm doesn't limit itself to what biology intended. It offers what biology *could have* intended, if biology had a bigger imagination and a smaller sense of propriety.

The technology is miraculous. The ethics are a void. RegenaForm doesn't ask why. They don't require therapy letters or diagnostic criteria or the careful gatekeeping that traditional medicine employs. They offer a menu, a price list, and a smile. The client chooses. The client pays. The body changes. What happens to the person inside it — the identity, the psychology, the sense of self that was built on the assumption that the body would stay the same — is not RegenaForm's department.

Their clinics have spread through every major city, quiet storefronts in unremarkable buildings, identified only by the chrome helix on frosted glass. The waiting lists are long. The prices are high but not prohibitive — calibrated precisely to be *just* affordable for a person who's desperate enough, *just* within reach of a person who can see the math working out if they earn enough, stream enough, transform enough, earn more, transform more.

The loop is the product. The escalation is the business model. The first procedure is free.

---

Into this world — this post-automation, algorithm-governed, attention-commodified, genetically-hackable world — step Eli Vance.

Twenty-two years old. Five-foot-nine. A hundred and forty pounds of skinny-fat neglect distributed across a frame that nature assembled with the enthusiasm of a substitute teacher filling in on a Friday. He has the kind of face that exists in the space between faces — not ugly enough to be memorable, not handsome enough to be noticed, just *there*, occupying a position in the visible spectrum that the human eye slides past the way it slides past wallpaper. Brown hair, usually greasy. Brown eyes, usually tired. Skin that can't decide if it's pale or sallow and has settled on both. Acne along the jawline that he's had since fifteen and will have until something changes.

He tried the normal path. One semester of college — computer science, the degree everyone said was "AI-proof," which turned out to be a joke that stopped being funny when the AI learned to code better than the professors teaching it. The financial aid fell through. The motivation followed. He applied for jobs — retail, food service, warehouse, delivery — and watched each one evaporate between the application and the interview, replaced by a kiosk or a bot or an app or a "restructuring" that was a euphemism for "we bought software." For six months he did gig work — food delivery on an e-bike in weather that tried to kill him, for an app that took 40% and offered no benefits and deactivated his account for a week when his rating dropped below 4.7 because a customer lied about a missing order.

Then he saw a YouTube video. A guy, roughly his age, roughly his situation, sitting in a room not unlike his, streaming a game to forty viewers and talking about how he made $600 last month. Six hundred dollars. For playing video games and talking. The number lodged in Eli's brain the way a bullet lodges in a wall — embedded, permanent, impossible to remove without pulling the whole structure apart.

He started streaming the next week. That was eight months ago.

He lives alone in a 280-square-foot studio apartment that costs $875 a month, which is $875 more than it's worth. The apartment contains: a mattress on the floor (found curbside, stain of unknown origin), a desk (also found curbside, one leg requires a cardboard shim), two monitors (one refurbished, one dying), a microphone (budget), a webcam (antique), a mini-fridge (humming), a hot plate, a microwave with a broken turntable, and a crack in the ceiling that grows a little every day, splitting the plaster from the kitchenette to his pillow like a fault line in cheap geology.

His channel, **EliPlays**, has 54 followers — most of whom followed during a brief raid six months ago and have never returned. His average viewership is between 8 and 15 people, a number that includes bots, accidental tab-leavers, and one dedicated troll named xX_DarkSlayer_Xx who appears daily, without fail, to inform Eli that he is terrible. His lifetime earnings from streaming total approximately $180. His most recent monthly income was $12.50. He plays *Gunmetal Requiem*, the biggest competitive shooter on Twitch, at the Silver rank — the vast, unmarketable middle of the skill spectrum where nobody watches because there's nothing to see.

He eats microwave burritos. He drinks Monster Energy. He sleeps at irregular hours on grey sheets that were once white. He picks at the skin around his thumbnails when he's anxious, which is always. He avoids mirrors. Not out of dysphoria — out of disappointment.

His social circle is small enough to list in a sentence: Marcus "Marc" Delano, his best friend since middle school, who works part-time at a phone repair shop and doesn't understand streaming and doesn't need to because he shows up with cheese puffs and sarcasm and the specific loyalty of a person who decided a long time ago that you were worth keeping. And Kira Nomura, a mid-tier streamer he met in a Discord server, who averages 800–1,200 viewers and earns more in a single session than Eli earns in a quarter, and who talks to him every day with a warmth he can't explain and won't examine because examining it would require asking why a successful, beautiful, razor-sharp 21-year-old woman chooses to spend her time on a guy who peaked at six viewers.

Eli has $47 in his checking account. He has no savings. No safety net. His mother works two jobs in another state and communicates through bimonthly Venmo deposits and texts that say "hope you're eating ok." No father — gone before the memory formed. No degree, no prospects, no plan beyond the next stream, which will be identical to the last stream, which produced nothing.

He is not suicidal. He is not in crisis. He is something worse: he is functional. He wakes up. He streams. He eats. He sleeps. He repeats. The machinery of his life operates at minimum capacity, producing just enough motion to qualify as alive, the way a pilot light produces just enough flame to qualify as lit. He is one of millions — a face in the browse-page graveyard, a thumbnail nobody clicks, a voice in an empty room, a person the algorithm has decided isn't worth surfacing — and the only thing that distinguishes him from the other millions is the specific shape of his desperation.

He doesn't know about RegenaForm yet. He doesn't know that a clinic four blocks from his apartment can reshape his face and rebuild his body and rewrite the equation that has defined his life since the day the algorithm decided he wasn't worth looking at. He doesn't know that the technology exists, that the door is there, that the first procedure is free, that the second will cost just enough to require a third, and the third will require a fourth, and the fourth will require everything.

He will.

---

This is the story of how a boy named Eli became a girl named Ellie, one upgrade at a time, for an audience that loved her and a mirror that couldn't decide who was looking back. It is a story about desire and technology and the price of being seen in a world that automated everything except loneliness. It is a story about a ceiling crack and a viewer count and a voice modulator and a kiss in a parked car and a wig in a closet and a number — $47 — that means everything and nothing depending on which side of the screen you're standing on.

It is a story about what happens when the system offers you a key and the only door it opens leads deeper inside yourself.

Let's dive into Eli's desperate world.
